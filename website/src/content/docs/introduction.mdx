blackhole-threats turns threat feeds into remotely triggered blackhole (RTBH) routes. It reads
local files and HTTP(S) feeds in plain text, JSON, JSONL or NDJSON, pulls out the IPv4 and IPv6
prefixes, summarises overlapping networks and announces the result to your BGP peers with a
community. Your routers match that community and route the traffic to a blackhole.

It's one Go binary with [GoBGP](https://github.com/osrg/gobgp) built in, so there's no separate
BGP daemon to run beside it. One YAML file holds both the GoBGP settings and the list of feeds,
and every feed is tagged with the community its routes carry. A feed with no community gets
`<local ASN>:666`, as long as the local ASN fits in 16 bits.

## What it doesn't do

blackhole-threats announces routes; it doesn't drop anything itself. What happens to the traffic
is up to the policy on your routers, and [Router policy](/blackhole-threats/guides/router-policy/)
has starting points for RouterOS. It does nothing with routes its peers send it, and it keeps no
state on disk: the routes it announces live in memory and are rebuilt from the feeds when it
starts.

It doesn't vet what the feeds say either, beyond refusing prefixes shorter than `/8` for IPv4 or
`/16` for IPv6, so a stray `0.0.0.0/0` can't blackhole everything. Any other valid prefix a feed
lists is announced, so only use feeds you trust and keep each one under its own community.

## How a refresh works

The daemon refreshes as soon as it starts, then every two hours by default, or straight away when
you send it `SIGUSR1` or `SIGHUP`. Each refresh does the same thing:

<Steps>

1. It groups the feeds by community. Feeds that share a community are read together and
   succeed or fail together.

2. It reads every feed in a group at the same time, takes one prefix from each line of a text
   feed or each object of a JSON feed, and turns bare addresses into `/32` or `/128` host routes.
   Prefixes shorter than the limits, `/8` and `/16` by default, are dropped.

3. It summarises each group: duplicates and prefixes inside a broader one go, and sibling
   prefixes merge into their parent. Two `/25`s that make up a `/24` become the `/24`.

4. If any feed in a group failed, including one that gave no prefixes at all, it keeps the routes
   that group had after the last refresh instead of building new ones.

5. It compares the new route table with what it's announcing, withdraws the routes that are
   gone or whose communities changed, and announces the new and changed ones. Unchanged routes
   aren't sent again.

</Steps>

[How it works](/blackhole-threats/how-it-works/) goes through each step with the log lines it
writes.

## Where it ships from

Everything is built and published from the repository: release archives for Linux `amd64`,
`arm64` and `arm`, Debian packages for `amd64`, `arm64` and `armhf` in a signed APT repository,
and a container image on GHCR for `linux/amd64` and `linux/arm64`. The project started as
[ebarkie/blackhole-threats](https://github.com/ebarkie/blackhole-threats) by Eric Barkie, which
established the idea of advertising threat-feed routes with GoBGP.
